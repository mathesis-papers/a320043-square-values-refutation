# OEIS A320043: A Pell Counterexample to a Square-Value Conjecture

## The question: when does a row sum become a square?

Imagine filling a $u\times u\times v$ rectangular box with identical cubes. All cubes are aligned with the box, have integer side length, and are as large as possible. Their side is the largest whole number dividing both $u$ and $v$, written $d=\gcd(u,v)$, so the box needs $(u/d)^2(v/d)$ cubes. For $u+v=4$, the three boxes use $9$, $1$ and $3$ cubes: the middle $2\times2\times2$ box needs just one cube of side two. Adding these three counts gives $13$, which is not a square. The question asks when the sum for a whole row is a square. This interpretation requires equal cubes; allowing unequal cubes would be a different problem.

For a positive integer $n$, arrange $n$ entries in a row. At position $k$ put

$$
X(n,k)=\frac{(n+1-k)^2k}{\gcd(n+1-k,k)^3},\qquad 1\le k\le n,
$$

and add the row:

$$
a(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X(n,k).$$

Here $\gcd(u,v)$ is the largest positive integer dividing both $u$ and $v$. For instance, $a(6)=196=14^2$, and $a(96)=7\,376\,656=2716^2$. In the COMMENTS of OEIS A320043, a conjecture posed in 2018 said that these were the only square values, apart from $a(1)=1$.

The conjecture has a very large counterexample. One additional square is $a(708158976)=144767444036350576^2$. The useful idea is not to add more than seven hundred million terms one by one. Instead, look at the row $n=p-1$ when $p$ is prime. Then $p-k$ and $k$ are coprime, so every gcd denominator is $1$ and the row becomes an elementary sum of a cubic polynomial.

## Why prime rows simplify

Let $p$ be prime and take $n=p-1$. If an integer divides both $p-k$ and $k$ for $1\le k<p$, it divides their sum $p$; since $p$ is prime and $k<p$, it must be $1$. Thus

$$
a(p-1)=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}(p-k)^2k.
$$

Expanding $(p-k)^2k$ and using the formulas for $\sum k$, $\sum k^2$, and $\sum k^3$ gives

$$
a(p-1)=\frac{p^2(p^2-1)}{12}.
$$

This is the turning point: a triangle defined with gcds becomes a simple quartic expression on a prime row.

For $p=708158977$,

$$
\begin{gathered}
p^2=501489136705686529,\\
12\cdot204427888^2=501489136705686528.
\end{gathered}
$$

so $p^2-12y^2=1$. Substituting into the prime-row identity gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
a(p-1)=\frac{p^2(p^2-1)}{12}=(py)^2,\\
py=144767444036350576.
\end{gathered}
$$

The paper also gives a Lucas primality certificate. It factors
$708158976=2^9\cdot3\cdot7^2\cdot97^2$ and uses powers of $5$ modulo $708158977$ together with four gcd conditions to rule out every smaller possible order. Hence $708158977$ is prime. These are finite integer calculations, rather than a visual guess that the number “looks prime.”

## Which prime rows can be squares?

A Pell solution immediately substitutes $p^2-12y^2=1$ into the row formula. For the converse, take a prime $p\ge5$ and suppose $a(p-1)=m^2$. Then
$12m^2=p^2(p^2-1)$. Since $p$ does not divide $12$, primality forces $p\mid m$; write $m=py$ and cancel $p^2$ to obtain the Pell equation $p^2-12y^2=1$.

The positive solutions of this Pell equation can be generated by descending. If $x^2-12y^2=1$ with $x,y>0$, set

$$
x'=7x-24y,\qquad y'=7y-2x.
$$

This preserves $x^2-12y^2$ and produces a smaller solution; repeating eventually reaches $(1,0)$. The inverse map is

$$
(x,y)=(7x'+24y',\,2x'+7y'),
$$

so every nonnegative solution comes from

$$
x_t+y_t\sqrt{12}=(7+2\sqrt{12})^t.
$$

Thus $x_0=1$, $x_1=7$, and
$x_{t+2}=14x_{t+1}-x_t$. This is an integer recurrence; no decimal approximation to $\sqrt{12}$ is needed.

The relevant recurrence values are:

| $t$ | $x_t$ | conclusion |
|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 7 | prime |
| 2 | 97 | prime |
| 3 | 1351 | $7\cdot193$ |
| 4 | 18817 | $31\cdot607$ |
| 5 | 262087 | $7\cdot37441$ |
| 6 | 3650401 | $97\cdot37633$ |
| 7 | 50843527 | $7\cdot7263361$ |
| 8 | 708158977 | prime |

The Pell solutions increase strictly, so nothing else below $x_8$ can qualify; $p=2,3$ give $a(1)=1$ and $a(2)=6$. This conclusion only concerns indices with a prime successor. A full classification of square values with composite successors, and the unrestricted least new square index, are outside the paper's scope.

Write $r_0=1$, $r_1=2$, and $r_{j+2}=4r_{j+1}-r_j$, so $r_j$ is the rational part of $(2+\sqrt3)^j$. Since $(2+\sqrt3)^2=7+2\sqrt{12}$, the even-indexed terms give the $x_t$ sequence; the recurrence $r_{j+2}=4r_{j+1}-r_j$ makes every later odd-indexed term even. If $d>1$ is odd, expanding $(7+2\sqrt{12})^{ds}$ shows that $x_s\mid x_{ds}$, so a prime term cannot have an index with an odd factor. Finally,
$x_{2t}=2x_t^2-1$ and $x_1=7$ give the residue $7$ modulo $30$ by induction.

## Two more identities in the triangle

The same table has structure beyond its square row sums. Multiplying a whole row instead of adding it always produces a perfect cube. If
$$B(N)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\frac{k}{\gcd(k,N)},$$
then the row product is $B(n+1)^3$. For a prime $p$, $B(p)=(p-1)!$: multiplying the positive integers below $p$ is enough. The middle entry of every odd-length row equals one.

For composite successors, the gcd denominators no longer all disappear. The paper groups entries with the same gcd: after dividing out that common factor, each group becomes a smaller coprime counting problem. Summing those groups gives a formula over the divisors of the successor. The exact formula and its proof are in the [mathematical appendix](statements.md).

## What the result does—and does not—say

The boundaries matter. The paper does not classify every square index, does not prove that Pell prime terms are infinite, and does not formalize Greathouse's stronger search bound. The existing formal proof rules out the three-index exclusion and uses a checked numerical witness. Its public conclusion does not state the later classification and minimality results. Those remain separate written results, each linked to its contribution task.
